Georgios Leventis

MSc Student

Program: MSc in Data Science and Information Technologies.

Thesis Title: Development of a Computational Framework for the Identification and Characterization of Network Motifs in Biological Networks.

Thesis Description: Biological networks provide a powerful representation of the complex interactions governing cellular processes, including gene regulation, protein interactions, signaling pathways, and molecular communication. Understanding the structural organization of these networks is essential for uncovering the mechanisms underlying biological function and identifying recurring patterns of molecular interactions. Network motifs are small, interconnected subgraphs that occur significantly more frequently in a biological network than expected under an appropriate random network model. These recurring structures can reveal characteristic organizational principles and provide insights into regulatory mechanisms, functional modules, and information processing within biological systems. However, the systematic identification of network motifs presents substantial computational challenges, particularly when analyzing large-scale networks with thousands of nodes and interactions. These challenges include efficient subgraph enumeration, identification of structurally equivalent patterns, and statistical assessment of motif significance. The purpose of this thesis is to develop a modular computational framework for the systematic discovery, quantification, and characterization of network motifs in biological networks. The framework will incorporate algorithms for enumerating small subgraphs, identifying their structural configurations through graph isomorphism techniques, and assessing their statistical enrichment against ensembles of randomized networks that preserve relevant topological properties. Particular emphasis will be placed on computational efficiency, scalability, and support for different network representations, including directed and undirected graphs. The project will also investigate alternative motif detection strategies, evaluate their computational performance, and implement visualization capabilities for exploring identified motifs and their distribution across biological networks. The developed framework will be evaluated using synthetic networks with known structural patterns and publicly available biological networks, including gene regulatory and protein–protein interaction networks. Where suitable functional annotations are available, the biological relevance of enriched motifs will also be investigated. Ultimately, the project aims to deliver a reproducible, extensible, and user-friendly computational tool that facilitates network motif analysis and supports the investigation of structural organization and functional relationships in complex biological systems.

Konstantina Alliou

MSc Student

Program: MSc in Data Science and Information Technologies.

Thesis Title: Exploring Association Rule Mining as an Alternative Approach for Measuring Gene–Gene Similarity in Single-Cell Transcriptomics Data.

Thesis Description: Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technologies enable the characterization of gene expression patterns at individual-cell resolution, providing unprecedented opportunities to investigate gene co-expression, functional relationships, and regulatory interactions. A fundamental task in transcriptomic data analysis is quantifying gene–gene similarity, traditionally achieved through statistical approaches such as Pearson and Spearman correlation. However, these methods primarily capture linear or monotonic relationships and may fail to identify more complex patterns of gene association. Association rule mining, originally developed to discover frequently co-occurring items in transactional datasets, offers an alternative framework for identifying relationships between genes based on their co-expression patterns across individual cells. By representing each cell as a transaction containing expressed genes, association rules can be extracted and evaluated using measures such as support, confidence, lift, and leverage. These measures may provide alternative perspectives on gene relationships that are not adequately captured by conventional correlation metrics. The purpose of this thesis is to systematically investigate the applicability of association rule mining as a proxy for measuring gene–gene similarity in high-dimensional scRNA-seq datasets. Particular emphasis will be placed on evaluating different strategies for transforming continuous gene expression measurements into transactional representations, investigating the effects of sparsity, gene expression prevalence, and cellular heterogeneity on association rule discovery. Furthermore, the study will explore approaches for transforming directional association rules into quantitative gene–gene similarity scores and compare their performance against established correlation and similarity measures. The evaluation will employ synthetic datasets with controlled dependency structures and publicly available single-cell transcriptomic datasets, assessing the ability of different approaches to recover known gene associations, identify biologically meaningful gene modules, and support downstream gene network reconstruction. Ultimately, the project aims to determine whether association rule-based measures can provide a reliable and computationally practical alternative or complement to conventional gene–gene similarity metrics in single-cell transcriptomics analysis.

Yannos Kotsovilis

MSc Student

Program: MSc in Data Science and Information Technologies.

Thesis Title: Systematic Benchmarking of Clustering Algorithms for Single-Cell RNA Sequencing Data.

Thesis Description: Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technologies enable the characterization of cellular heterogeneity at unprecedented resolution, providing valuable insights into cellular populations, functional states, and complex biological processes. A fundamental step in scRNA-seq data analysis is clustering, which aims to identify groups of cells exhibiting similar transcriptional profiles. Although numerous clustering algorithms have been developed, their performance can vary considerably depending on dataset characteristics, including dimensionality, sparsity, sequencing depth, batch effects, and cell population composition. Furthermore, the absence of reliable ground-truth annotations in many experimental datasets complicates the objective assessment of clustering accuracy and biological relevance. The purpose of this thesis is to conduct a systematic comparative evaluation of established clustering algorithms, including graph-based, partitioning, hierarchical, and density-based approaches, using publicly available single-cell transcriptomic datasets and synthetic data with controlled properties. The evaluation will investigate clustering accuracy, stability, sensitivity to hyperparameter selection, computational efficiency, and the ability to distinguish rare or transcriptionally similar cell populations. Performance will be assessed using complementary internal and external validation metrics, where reference annotations are available, alongside biological validation based on established cell-type markers. Particular emphasis will be placed on understanding how preprocessing, dimensionality reduction, neighborhood graph construction, and clustering resolution influence the resulting cellular partitions. The project aims to establish a reproducible benchmarking framework and provide evidence-based recommendations for selecting appropriate clustering strategies across diverse single-cell transcriptomics applications.

Konstantinos Kapelios

MSc Student

Program: MSc in Data Science and Information Technologies.

Thesis Title: Enabling reproducible management of high-throughput sequencing analysis results and their metadata.

Thesis Description: Due to the large scale of high-throughput sequencing data generation, the community has established standards that promote Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable (FAIR) science. However, critical obstacles remain since best practices are not consistently enforced, with essential information being fragmented across methods, supplementary materials, and public repositories. When attempting to reproduce scientific findings or reuse published data, researchers often avoid analyzing sequencing data from the ground up. Instead, they prefer to use post-alignment information (e.g. gene expression matrices). However, existing repositories and workflow-oriented solutions rarely provide a single, portable, queryable resource that integrates this information with the metadata required for downstream reuse. The purpose of this project is to establish bMINTY, a locally deployed web application with an intuitive user interface, for structured management of post-alignment workflow data outputs. The app supports metadata for studies, assays, and analysis assets, including workflows, genome annotation versions, and cell-level entities for single-cell assays. Users may export query results in RO-Crate format, providing machine readable data packages and metadata. These packages can be included as supplementary material with each publication, accompanied by analysis code deposited in public repositories for downstream ad hoc analyses. Together, these practices can promote transparency, efficient FAIR-aligned reuse of published post-alignment data.

Michalis Antoniadis

MSc Student

Program: MSc in Data Science and Information Technologies.

Thesis Title: raw2ready: A Domain-Adaptable Multi-Agent LLM System for Evidence-Grounded Data Analysis with an Application to Biomanufacturing.

Thesis Description: Research in almost every experimental field lean on evidence that is scattered across sources that were never meant to work together: raw instrument readings, curated reference databases, published literature, and knowledge that lives only on the open web. Pulling those fragments into a single trustworthy answer is slow, and it is easy to get wrong. Large language models promise to speed this up, but on their own they invent facts and cite papers that do not exist, which makes them unsafe precisely where accuracy matters most. This thesis presents raw2ready, a system built to handle both halves of that problem in one place. The first half is a no-code data-preparation pipeline that loads, merges, transforms, and statistically profiles heterogeneous experimental files. The second is a layer of specialized agents: an orchestrator decides which one a question needs, each agent gathers evidence from its own source, and a final agent weaves their findings into a single grounded answer. One rule runs through the whole system every number the user sees is either retrieved from a live source or computed openly from their own data, and anything that cannot be sourced is labelled as unavailable rather than guessed. The problem is universal, but the system is shown at work on biomanufacturing, a domain demanding enough to make fabrication costly. The evaluation goes past “does it run”: for each agent it checks whether the steps it is supposed to follow produce the right answer, and it traces how faithfully the gathered evidence reaches the language model. Because neither the pipeline nor the agent contract knows anything about biology, the same design carries over to other data-heavy fields by swapping in different agents.

Foteini Papageorgiou

MSc Student

Program: MSc in Data Science and Information Technologies.

Thesis Title: Interpretable Graph Neural Networks for Multi-Omics.

Thesis Description: Cell identity is established and maintained by transcription factors acting through cis-regulatory elements that are accessible only in the cells that use them. However, models that classify single cells from their molecular profiles rarely explain the regulatory logic behind their decisions. This thesis introduces NUTMEG, a heterogeneous graph attention network that classifies cells over an explicit transcription factor–cis-regulatory element–gene scaffold constructed from paired RNA and ATAC measurements. The model is designed so that every stage, from message passing to classification, remains traceable to individual regulatory interactions and genes. It was evaluated on 2,320 peripheral blood mononuclear cells spanning nine populations in three configurations: a multiome configuration using both modalities, a transcriptome-only configuration matched to the same cells and genes, and a transcriptome-only pipeline with broader gene coverage, together with a linear support vector machine baseline. The central result is that predictive accuracy and interpretive fidelity are dissociable properties of the same family of models, and that the ranking of the configurations reverses depending on the criterion considered. The multiome configuration is the weakest classifier, reaching a macro F1 of 0.7830, compared with 0.8362 for the matched configuration and 0.9341 for the full transcriptome-only pipeline. Under a threshold-free rank test against three independent marker sources, however, it prioritises known markers more strongly than any other configuration, significantly in all nine cell types and for all three sources. It is also the only configuration that preserves the coreceptor-defined lineage structure of the T-cell compartment rather than reorganising it around shared effector programmes. Because the multiome and matched configurations differ in exactly one respect, this reversal is attributable to the chromatin-anchored representation itself, which acts as a biologically informed structural prior: it constrains each gene’s score to be assembled from motif-supported elements that are open in that cell, at a cost in accuracy but with a gain in the biological coherence of the resulting explanations. Regulatory paths reconstructed from the model’s attention weights and tested against a permutation null recover lineage-appropriate regulator sets for every class. In naive B cells, these sets converge on a regulon inferred independently by an unrelated method. Individual target assignments, however, are limited by the sparsity of the element-to-gene layer, by the family-level resolution of motif models, and by the absence of directional regulatory information. The chromatin-anchored formulation is therefore the weaker instrument for labelling cells, but the stronger one for explaining them.

Panagiotis Xiropotamos

MSc Student

Program: MSc in Data Science and Information Technologies.

Thesis Title: Development of Explainable Deep Learning Methods for Deciphering Transcription Factor Dynamics.

Thesis Description: Living cells require a differential activation of transcription factors (TFs) to establish cell type-discriminating gene expression. Binding affinity, timing, and combinatorial activity with other TFs at enhancer and promoter regions affect the expressed gene product at each state and while some of those associations have been deciphered through experimentation, a comprehensive understanding of the dynamic TF interactions during each cell state remains elusive. In this study, we utilized chromatin affinity data via ChIP-seq3 protocol for TCF1 factor, which encompasses regulatory regions such as enhancers and promoters, where the factor binds and regulates gene expression, on a genome-wide scale for two cell types, Double positive T cells, which naturally produce TCF1, and NIH3T3 fibroblasts, which are induced to express TCF1. The binding profile was used as input to an in-house developed Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model, trained to distinguish whether a sequence is accessible by TCF1 to either of the cell types, to therefore infer any biological background concerning the two systems. Our hypothesis posited that transcription factor motifs, pivotal in either state, underlie the key distinguishing features among diverse regulatory elements in the two different biological systems. Interpreting the first convolutional layers and especially the filter kernels provided us with insights into what the model grasped during training, to associate DNA sequences to cell types. The novel-developed interpretability modules provide insights into the TFs implicated in the modeling process, how important they are in the decision-making, their association with the studied systems, and most importantly the interplay of those learned representations, inferring the dynamics of those TFs among the two cell types. Among the results, binding sites of known cooperators of TCF1, such as JUNB and FOSL1 have been found among others, while the application of the interpretability modules on model snapshots during training has provided an enhanced view of the training process. By applying artificial intelligence to such complex biological questions of TF dynamic activity, we show the modeling capabilities of convolutional neural networks to identify TF binding sites and associate them to cell types only from the sequence itself, enabling us to further inspect the explainability opportunities that those models have to offer.

Haris Manousaki

MSc Student

Program: MSc in Data Science and Information Technologies.

Thesis Title: Integrating Spatial Transcriptomics in single cell resolution with Explainable Machine Learning for Enhanced Insights in Lung cancer Biology.

Thesis Description: Diseases such as cancer or autoimmune disorders have a molecular background. Specifically, the human organism consists of cells which, despite their identical genetic material are assigned to different groups based on their gene expression profiles. Therefore, mutations in important genes can disrupt normal cell behavior which can sometimes lead to cancer. Trustworthy analytical tools are in serious need, as they can assist in rapid diagnosis or therapy, with the identification of new targets, aligning with the scope of precision medicine for better and personalized therapy. To this end, spatially resolved transcriptomics technologies (SRT) combined with explainable machine learning (ML) can assist in uncovering the molecular background. SRT provides transcriptional activity and its positional context, and shares, except for their additional spatial dimension, many common characteristics with scRNA-seq and challenges such as the marker-gene-based cell type annotation approach, widely used. Thus, the integration of SRT with ML despite its great potential to aid the field of precision medicine, faces many uncertainties making it difficult for an approach to transition from theoretical research to clinical application. For its applicability, a method must first establish trustworthiness which includes pinpointing uncertainty, implementing necessary improvements, and integrating explainability to understand the decisions made by ML models. In this thesis, we attempt to address some of these points. Specifically, a central goal of this thesis is to observe the uncertainty inherent in the cell classification process arising from the golden standard, marker-gene annotation approach. Thus, our objective is to train Machine Learning models for cell classification by integrating SRT gene expression data in single-cell resolution, with both the standard annotation approach, using the Seurat package, and an enhanced version provided by Nanostring. Based on the comparison of classification performance between models trained using these two distinct cell labeling methods, we hope to uncover any uncertainty associated with the conventional annotation approach. The second part of this thesis, focuses on uncovering biological insights, mostly in terms of cell-to-cell interactions which we believe hide significant information. Those insights could assist the field of precision medicine and may even pinpoint genes that could eventually lead to a more enhanced annotation process. Therefore, we decided to employ Shannon entropy to automatically identify boundaries of different cellular groups and based on those to search for differences between boundaries and non-boundaries in terms of misclassification ratio. The results for the first part of the analysis revealed, based on unseen data, a significant performance drop by approximately 28% in Seurat labels, highlighting the inherent uncertainty of the marker-gene approach. For the second chapter of the analysis, contrary to our initial hypothesis, the boundaries of cell types and niches seemed to be well-defined while, higher misclassification rates were observed in grid regions associated with stroma and myeloid-enriched stroma, particularly near tumor stroma boundaries. Additionally, smaller grid analysis also revealed high misclassification rates at these boundaries. This might suggest that misclassification and gene expression changes may be linked to the tumor’s influence on nearby cells or structural regions involved in defense. These observations unveil the potential of our method to extract biological insights from SRT data and leverage explainable ML to explore the tumor microenvironment.
